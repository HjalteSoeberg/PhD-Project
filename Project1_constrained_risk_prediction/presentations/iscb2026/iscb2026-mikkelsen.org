*** Setting: competing risks
Assume we are in a competing risks scenario and want to predict the risk of getting diagnosed with T2D and the risk of dying, at some fixed timepoint $\tau$. Denote:
\vfill
- $T$ as the time between time0 and the date of an event
- $D\in\{1,2\}$ as the cause of the event
- $X = (X^1,...,X^p)$ as a p-dimensional vector of baseline covariates, which can both be numeric or categorical.
\vfill
Additionally we assume that the event time $T$ is right censored at the random time $C$, where $P(C>\tau\vert X)>0$ almost surely. Denote $\tilde{T} = \min(T,C)$ and $\tilde{D} = \Delta D$ as the observed time and event, where $\Delta = 1\{T\leq C\}$.


*** The idea: fit both risks to the prediction horizon
Let $\tilde{F}_j(t|x) = P(\tilde{T}\leq t, \tilde{D} = j|x)$ and $o_{ij}(\tau) = 1(\tilde{D}_i= j,\tilde{T}_i\leq \tau)$
#+begin_export latex
\begin{block}{Brier-score loss function}
\[
BSE=
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=0}^{2}
\{\tilde{F}_j(\tau\mid X_i)-o_{ij}(\tau)\}^{2}.
\]
\end{block}
#+end_export
\pause

- One loss estimates the two cause-specific models *simultaneously*.
# - $\Theta$ encodes coherence: $F_j\geq0$ and $F_0+F_1+F_2=1$.

Here the sum starts at $j = 0$ which is a method for handling right censoring \cite{munch}.

*** COMMENT Maximum likelihood
# this is not the full likelihood, missing censoring
Assume we have $N$ observations of $(\tilde{T},\tilde{D}, X)$. Using that the survival function is a product of the individual cause-specific survival functions, $S(t_i|x_i) = \prod S_j(t_i|x_i)$, we can write the likelihood as
#+begin_export latex
\begin{align*}
L = \prod_{j = 1}^2\prod_{i = 1}^N\lambda_{j}(t_i|x_i)^{d_{ij}}\exp(-\Lambda_j(t_i|x_i)),
\end{align*}
#+end_export
where $d_{ij} = 1\{\tilde{D}_i = j\}$. \pause This means that the likelihood function is a product of 2 likelihoods, one for each cause. We can then maximize each cause-specific likelihood function seperately by choosing some parametric form for the baseline hazards.


*** The Cox-exponential model

For causes $j\in\{0,1,2\}$, assume constant baseline hazards:
\[
\lambda_j(t\mid x)=\lambda_{0j}\exp(x^\top\beta_j),\qquad \lambda_{0j}>0.
\]
Let $\tilde{A}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{2}\lambda_{0j}\exp(x^\top\beta_j)$. Then
\[
\tilde{F}_j(\tau\mid x)=\frac{\lambda_{0j}\exp(x^\top\beta_j)}{\tilde{A}(x)}
\{1-\exp[-\tau \tilde{A}(x)]\}.
\]

All parameter sets $(\beta_0,\lambda_{00})$, $(\beta_1,\lambda_{01})$ and $(\beta_2,\lambda_{02})$ are
estimated simultaniously by minimizing the Brier score loss.

*** Brier score estimation
Assuming Cox-proportional hazards model and constant baseline hazards for all causes, our estimator becomes:

#+begin_export latex
\begin{align*}
\hat{\theta}_{BSE}=&\arg\min_{\theta}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=0}^{2}\\
&\{\frac{\lambda_{0j}\exp(x_i^\top\beta_j)}{\tilde{A}(x_i)}\{1-\exp[-\tau \tilde{A}(x_i)]\}-o_{ij}(\tau)\}^{2}.
\end{align*}
#+end_export
\pause \vfill
Cause specific risk prediction can then be obtained by substituting into $F_j(\tau|x) = P(T\leq \tau, D = j|x)$. Let $\hat{A}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{2}\hat{\lambda}_{0j}\exp(x^\top\hat{\beta}_j)$
#+begin_export latex
\begin{align*}
F_j(\tau|x) = \frac{\hat{\lambda}_{0j}\exp(x^\top\hat{\beta}_j)}{\hat{A}(x)}
\{1-\exp[-\tau \hat{A}(x)]\}, \text{ for }j\in\{1,2\}
\end{align*}
#+end_export

*** Why estimate like this?

Estimating both cause-specific hazards this way:
- targets calibration and discrimination directly at the prediction horizon;
- performs well in non-proportional hazards data;
- prevents impossible pairs of predictions;
- produces a portable parametric learner for use outside protected enviroments.
\pause
\vfill
Choosing a simple parametric model makes sense as we only fit the model for one specific timepoint, so we don't need the baseline hazard to vary over time.


* The role of follow-up after five years

*** Artificial censoring at the prediction horizon

Artificial censoring replaces the observed follow-up by
\[
\widetilde T_i^{(5)}=\min(\widetilde T_i,5),\qquad
\widetilde D_i^{(5)}=\widetilde D_i1\{\widetilde T_i\leq5\}.
\]
It removes all event-time information after the five-year horizon. This affects the estimates of Cox models fitted using likelihood methods.

#+begin_export latex
\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
\begin{block}{It can lose predictive power}
If proportional hazards is correct, later events inform the same $\beta_j$ that
governs hazards before five years. Discarding them wastes information.
\end{block}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
\begin{block}{It can gain predictive power}
If effects change after five years, an untruncated Cox fit compromises across
periods and can learn the wrong effect for the five-year target.
\end{block}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
#+end_export

*** COMMENT The Cox-exponential Brier-score estimator changes the question

Likelihood-based Cox estimation asks:
\begin{center}
``Which hazard parameters best explain all observed event times?''
\end{center}
The proposed estimator asks:
\begin{center}
``Which two models best predict each person's state at $\tau=5$ years?''
\end{center}

The Brier target is inherently horizon-specific. Follow-up after $\tau$ does not contribute to the outcome, while censoring before $\tau$ still
has to be handled by the censoring model/weights.
